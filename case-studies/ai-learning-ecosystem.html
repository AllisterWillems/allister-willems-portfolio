Enterprise AI Learning Ecosystem

A company-wide learning ecosystem that translated emerging AI concepts into practical workforce capability, supported here by public-safe examples of the strategy, learning architecture, and application guidance behind it.

Organization
Roku
Industry
Media technology / streaming
Company size
3,000+ employees globally
Focus areas
AI enablement, learning systems, workflow adoption

Organizational Context

AI tools and AI-assisted workflows were evolving quickly, while non-technical employees needed shared vocabulary, practical examples, and responsible-use guidance. The work addressed a growing capability gap between early adopters and employees who needed clearer entry points into AI-enabled work.

Environmental Factors

  • Rapidly changing AI tools and concepts.
  • Need for non-technical AI fluency across functions.
  • Growing need to connect AI literacy to real workflows rather than abstract concepts.

The Challenge

The challenge was not simply explaining AI terminology. The larger problem was helping employees understand what AI could do, where it could fail, and how to apply it responsibly in the context of their actual work.

Key Friction Points

  • AI concepts were changing faster than formal learning structures.
  • Non-technical users needed practical mental models, not engineering-first explanations.
  • Adoption required behavior, workflow, and judgment, not just tool awareness.

Strategy & Systems Design

The approach was to build a broad AI learning ecosystem that translated complex concepts into practical workforce capability. Topics included LLMs, prompting, RAG, MCP, agents, Copilot, Claude, Glean, AI Hub adoption, and AI-assisted workflows.

Systems Built

  • Company-wide AI literacy and enablement content.
  • Practical learning around prompting, workflow thinking, and AI-assisted work.
  • A proposed 2026 AI learning strategy focused on non-technical fluency, human oversight of AI workflows, ambassador models, governance alignment, and behavior-based indicators.

Why This Approach

A tool-focused approach would not have addressed the real adoption problem. Employees needed shared language, relevant examples, workflow context, and a way to reason about responsible use and AI failure modes.

Implementation & Rollout

The company-wide curriculum was built as an enterprise enablement system rather than a single training artifact. A later 2026 strategy extended that work into proposed priorities and dependencies. It is included as evidence of strategic thinking, not represented as implemented after departure.

To protect internal materials, this public version focuses on the design approach, representative artifacts, and verified outcome signals rather than implementation-specific details.

Content / Asset Types

  • Learning paths and modular learning content.
  • Workflow-oriented examples and use cases.
  • Strategy and adoption framing for broader AI capability development.

Outcomes & Signals

The curriculum was adopted company-wide and helped position AI adoption as a workforce capability challenge rather than a tool-access issue.

Quantitative Signals

  • Designed for a 3,000+ employee enterprise environment.

Qualitative Outcomes

  • Helped close non-technical AI fluency gaps.
  • Created practical language for AI-enabled work, prompting, and workflow thinking.
  • Supported a broader shift toward responsible AI adoption and workforce capability.

Lessons Learned

AI enablement is strongest when it starts from real work. Shared vocabulary matters, but adoption depends on workflow relevance, manager reinforcement, and practical judgment around when and how to use AI.
